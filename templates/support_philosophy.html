My Enterprise IT Support Philosophy

Enterprise IT support is not simply about fixing computers or closing tickets. It is about restoring productivity, taking ownership, earning user trust, understanding the environment behind the incident, and building solutions that remain supportable as the organization grows.

Over the years, I have learned that the best technical solutions often come from understanding not only the technology, but also the people, history, dependencies, and relationships surrounding it.

Be flexible with the user, consistent with the system, take ownership of the problem, understand the environment behind it, bring the right people together, and build solutions that scale.

01Restore the User’s Ability to Work

The first question should not always be:

“How do I fix this device?”

It should also be:

“What does the user need to accomplish?”

If a user cannot print, their immediate need may not actually be to repair a particular printer. They need to print.

Connecting them to another available printer may restore their productivity immediately while the failed printer is tracked and repaired separately.

A workaround can therefore be a valid incident resolution when it safely restores the user's ability to work. The underlying problem should still be documented and addressed.

Restore productivity first. Then address the underlying problem properly.

02Take Ownership Instead of Simply Transferring

Improving First Contact Resolution does not mean every technician must know everything.

It means the technician receiving the issue should take ownership whenever possible.

Before transferring a ticket, I believe in using the resources around me: documentation, knowledge bases, previous incidents, and the expertise of other team members.

A Tier II or Tier III technician should not necessarily have to take ownership of a ticket simply because their knowledge is needed. They can assist the original technician behind the scenes while that technician continues working with the user.

This reduces unnecessary transfers, improves the user experience, and allows technicians to learn from one another.

Collaborate before transferring.

03Listen for the Problem Behind the Ticket

What the user says is broken and what the user actually needs are not always the same thing.

Good troubleshooting begins by listening carefully, understanding the user's workflow, and determining what outcome they are trying to achieve.

A ticket describes a symptom. A conversation often reveals the actual impact.

The goal is not merely to close the ticket. The goal is to understand the business need and find the most appropriate way to restore the user's ability to work.

04Trust Is a Troubleshooting Tool

When users trust their IT support team, they speak up.

They ask more questions, mention smaller problems, explain what changed, and share information that may never appear in the original ticket.

A conversation about one problem may reveal that another application has also been behaving strangely, that several colleagues have experienced the same issue, or that the problem has been occurring intermittently for weeks.

Those conversations help IT see the bigger picture and can reveal patterns before they become larger incidents.

Customer service and technical troubleshooting are therefore not separate skills.

Trust creates better information. Better information leads to better troubleshooting.

05See the Environment, Not Just the Ticket

A ticket is rarely just a ticket.

Every incident carries context: previous changes, related systems, user workflows, dependencies, other incidents, and the history of the environment.

The more incidents you work on, the more those connections begin to appear.

An issue that initially looks unrelated may connect to a previous deployment. That deployment may explain another incident. Several incidents may eventually point toward the same application, infrastructure component, configuration change, or support team.

Every ticket adds context.

Over time, troubleshooting becomes less about looking at individual incidents in isolation and more about understanding the environment that produced them.

The goal is not simply to understand the ticket in front of you.

Understand the environment behind the ticket.

06Expertise Is Understanding How Things Connect

No support engineer can know everything.

Experience is not simply accumulating answers. It is also learning how systems, teams, technologies, and incidents connect.

Sometimes I may not immediately know the technical answer, but understanding the history of the environment and the responsibilities of the teams around it gives me a path toward finding that answer.

A symptom may require knowledge from an application developer. Another may require someone familiar with software deployment. Another may need network, hardware, security, or identity expertise.

Knowing when those connections matter is part of troubleshooting.

Expertise is not knowing everything. It is knowing how things connect.

07Bring the Right Knowledge Into the Room

Good troubleshooting is often collaborative.

The support technician does not always need to be the person with the deepest knowledge of every component. Sometimes the most valuable contribution is understanding the problem well enough to bring the right people together.

One person may understand the application. Another may know the deployment history. Someone else may understand the hardware or network behavior.

When those pieces of knowledge come together, what appeared to be several unrelated symptoms can suddenly become one understandable problem.

You do not need to be the smartest person in the room. Sometimes your job is to bring the right knowledge into the room.

08Avoid Unnecessary Exceptions

In a standardized enterprise environment, I try to avoid solving problems by making one computer permanently different from all the others.

If endpoints were originally built from the same image, Autopilot deployment, or configuration baseline, I first want to understand why one device is behaving differently.

One-off patches, unsupported software, unnecessary local administrator rights, and unique configurations may solve today's ticket while creating tomorrow's support problem.

There are legitimate exceptions, but they should be intentional, documented, approved, secure, and supportable.

A good enterprise solution should move an endpoint toward the standard—not further away from it.

09Build Solutions That Scale

When I find a solution, I ask myself:

Would I be comfortable applying this fix to 100 computers?

A strong enterprise solution should be repeatable, documented, secure, and scalable.

If the same problem appears on ten or one hundred devices tomorrow, the solution should not depend on one technician remembering a special trick used on one computer.

The solution should become organizational knowledge.

10Fix One. Confirm. Communicate. Then Scale.

When the same issue affects multiple endpoints, I prefer to validate the solution on one device first rather than immediately making changes across every affected system.

Once the fix is successful, I confirm the result with the user.

Then I communicate the proven solution before expanding the repair.

Communication itself can accelerate technical recovery.

When users know that the root cause has been identified and a fix has already been proven, they are often more willing to make their computers available, coordinate with colleagues, and help IT reach other affected users.

Prove the fix → Confirm the result → Communicate → Build confidence → Gain cooperation → Scale the recovery

11Make Users Partners in Support

Enterprise IT works best when support is not something IT does to users, but something IT accomplishes with them.

Users understand their workflows better than anyone. IT understands the technology.

A trusted user may help identify other affected employees, make systems available for troubleshooting, communicate with colleagues, or provide operational context that IT would otherwise never see.

This creates a positive cycle:

Support builds trust.
Trust creates communication.
Communication creates context.
Context reveals connections.
Connections lead to better solutions.

The relationship itself becomes part of the support infrastructure.

12Collaboration Has Two Directions

Strong enterprise support depends on two kinds of collaboration: collaboration with end users and collaboration within the support team.

Collaborate with Users

Build enough trust that users become partners in the recovery process. Keep them informed, listen to what they observe, and, when appropriate, enable them to help identify affected colleagues, coordinate access to devices, and share accurate information.

Good communication does more than improve the user experience. It can make the overall recovery faster.

Collaborate Within the Support Team

Ticket ownership should never mean isolation. The technician who owns an incident remains responsible for driving it toward resolution, but the knowledge of the entire team should be available to help them get there.

When a technician asks for help, the goal should not automatically be to take the ticket away from them. Whenever practical, help them understand the problem and complete the resolution themselves.

Over time, this creates a support organization where knowledge is shared, technicians become more capable, and the quality of a solution does not depend on who happens to receive the ticket.

The technician owns the ticket. The team owns the success.

A mature support team should be able to produce a consistent, repeatable, and supportable solution regardless of which technician receives the incident.
